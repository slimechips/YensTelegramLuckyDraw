# 100 Advanced Lateral Thinking Puzzles

These are the harder ones: multi-step situations, counter-intuitive logic, physics and maths twists, and classic paradoxes. Many have a first obvious answer that is wrong.

**How to use:** think it through (or play it in a group), then click **Answer** to check.

*All puzzles are written in my own words. Some are classics that circulate widely; many are original.*

## Time, Place & Travel (1–8)

**1.** A man has his birthday dinner in Tokyo. Later that same calendar date he has another birthday dinner in Los Angeles, after an overnight flight from Tokyo that landed mid-afternoon. He isn't lying about the date and nobody has time-travelled. How is it possible?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>He flew east across the International Date Line. Tokyo is about 17 hours ahead of Los Angeles, so after a roughly 10-hour flight the calendar is still on the same date, and he gets to relive his birthday.</details>

**2.** An astronaut sees the sun rise 16 times within a single 24-hour day, with no clocks altered. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The International Space Station orbits Earth about every 90 minutes, so it passes through roughly 16 sunrises and sunsets per day.</details>

**3.** A night nurse is on a 12-hour shift. On one night of the year she works it for 13 real hours without staying a minute late. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It's the night the clocks go back at the end of daylight saving time. The clock hour repeats, so a 12-hour clock shift lasts 13 real hours.</details>

**4.** A hotel guest checks in on Thursday 29 December and checks out the next morning on Saturday 31 December, having stayed one night. Nothing is wrong with the calendar of the hotel. What happened?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The country skipped a whole day. In 2011 Samoa moved to the other side of the International Date Line and Friday 30 December never happened there.</details>

**5.** A hiker walks 1 km south, then 1 km east, then 1 km north, and finds herself exactly where she started. Name two different kinds of starting places where this works.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>(1) The North Pole: south, 'east' round a tiny circle, and north back. (2) Any point 1 km north of a circle of latitude near the South Pole whose circumference is 1 km (or 1/2 km, 1/3 km...). There are infinitely many such rings.</details>

**6.** Two trains 300 km apart head toward each other, each at 100 km/h. A bird starts from the front of one and flies back and forth between them at 150 km/h until they meet. How far does the bird fly?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>225 km. The trains close the gap at 200 km/h, so they meet after 1.5 hours; the bird flies 150 × 1.5 = 225 km. There's no need to sum the back-and-forth trips.</details>

**7.** A monk climbs a mountain path, starting at 6 a.m. and arriving at 6 p.m., at varying speeds. He sleeps at the top, then descends the same path the next day from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., again at varying speeds. Must there be a spot he passes at the same time of day on both journeys?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. Imagine two monks, one going up and one coming down on the same day. They must cross somewhere, and that spot at that time is the answer.</details>

**8.** A traveller is thrilled to watch the sun rise in the west. Where is she?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>On Venus, which rotates backwards (retrograde), so the Sun rises in the west.</details>

## Crime & Detection (9–16)

**9.** A witness swears she saw the murderer leave the café at 7:20 by the clock on the wall. Police find the man left at 4:40. The witness is honest and her eyesight is perfect. What went wrong?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>She read the clock in a mirror, where the face is reversed. 4:40 reflected in a mirror looks like roughly 7:20.</details>

**10.** A wife and husband share a pot of poisoned tea. She pours a cup for each of them. He dies and she remains perfectly well. She had not taken an antidote. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The poison was smeared only inside his cup. The tea itself was harmless.</details>

**11.** Snow begins falling at midnight. In the morning a single line of footprints leads to a house, and there are none leading away. A man is dead inside, killed around 9 p.m. Who left the prints?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The person who found the body, arriving in the morning. The killer left before the snow started, so no tracks remained.</details>

**12.** A burglar breaks into a house guarded by a large, loud dog. The dog doesn't make a sound all night. What does this suggest?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The intruder was someone the dog knew, such as a family member or a regular visitor.</details>

**13.** A suspect produces a will, dated 29 February 2019, which he says was signed by the victim. The detective declares it forged without any handwriting analysis. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>2019 wasn't a leap year, so 29 February 2019 didn't exist.</details>

**14.** An art dealer sells a painting 'by a famous artist, painted in 1890'. An expert immediately suspects it's fake after a lab test finds a specific white pigment in the paint. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The pigment (such as titanium white) wasn't commercially available until the 20th century, so it couldn't have been used in 1890.</details>

**15.** A shopkeeper says the thief was wearing a red coat. A passer-by says it was green. Both are honest, sighted, and describing the same person at the same time. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The coat was reversible. The thief flipped it between the two sightings.</details>

**16.** A woman testifies that she identified the attacker clearly by bright full-moon light at 2 a.m. on the night of the crime. The detective knows she's wrong about the light. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Astronomical records show that night was a new moon, so there was no moonlight.</details>

## Science & Physics (17–24)

**17.** A rock sits in a rowing boat floating in a swimming pool. The rock is thrown overboard and sinks. Does the pool's water level rise, fall, or stay the same?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It falls. In the boat the rock displaces a volume of water equal to its weight; underwater it displaces only its own (smaller) volume.</details>

**18.** An ice cube floats in a full-to-the-brim glass of water. When it melts, does the glass overflow?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. The floating ice already displaces exactly its own weight in water, so the level stays the same.</details>

**19.** A plane flies from A to B and back. Is the round trip quicker, slower, or the same with a steady wind blowing from A to B, compared with still air?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Slower. The time lost against the wind is more than the time gained with it.</details>

**20.** Two identical candles are lit at the same time. One burns out in 4 hours, the other in 3 hours. After how long is one candle exactly four times as long as the other?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>36/13 hours, about 2 hours 46 minutes. Solve 1 − t/4 = 4(1 − t/3).</details>

**21.** Two identical coins lie flat on a table, touching. One is held still while the other rolls around its edge, without slipping, until it returns to its starting point. How many times does the rolling coin rotate?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Twice: once for the distance it travels and once more because it goes around the other coin.</details>

**22.** A flat metal plate has a round hole in it. When the plate is heated, does the hole get bigger, smaller, or stay the same?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Bigger. Heating expands all dimensions, including the hole, as if it were made of the same metal.</details>

**23.** A beaker of water sits on a scale. You dip a finger in without touching the glass. What happens to the reading?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It increases by the weight of the water your finger displaces. The water pushes up on your finger and your finger pushes down on the water.</details>

**24.** In a lift whose cable has snapped, you drop a coin. What does it look like it does?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It floats beside you, since you and the coin fall at the same rate.</details>

## Why Would They? (25–38)

**25.** Many ambulances have their name painted on the front in mirror writing. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>So drivers ahead can read it correctly in their rear-view mirror.</details>

**26.** A bridge engineer orders marching soldiers to break step before crossing. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Marching in step can set up resonance that makes the bridge sway dangerously.</details>

**27.** A pilot has two perfect eyes but flies with an eyepatch over one in a dark, high-stakes mission. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The covered eye stays dark-adapted, so when lights flash or cockpit lights are switched on, he can still see in the dark with it.</details>

**28.** Why does a theatre leave a single bulb burning on an empty stage all night?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Safety: so the next person who enters doesn't fall into the orchestra pit. (There's also a theatrical tradition of the 'ghost light'.)</details>

**29.** The clock at a railway station is deliberately set two minutes fast, and everybody knows it. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It nudges passengers to hurry and be punctual.</details>

**30.** Airlines ask passengers to raise window blinds for take-off and landing. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Eyes adjust to the outside light, and crew and passengers can see hazards and fire outside if they must evacuate.</details>

**31.** A scuba diver with lots of air left ascends slowly and stops for minutes at three metres below the surface. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A decompression or safety stop lets dissolved nitrogen leave the body gradually, avoiding decompression sickness.</details>

**32.** Why do some vineyards plant rose bushes at the ends of their vine rows?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Roses catch mildew and certain pests earlier than vines do, so they work as an early-warning system.</details>

**33.** Why do casinos typically have no clocks and no windows?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>To help players lose track of time.</details>

**34.** Railway track beds are made of sharp, jagged stones rather than smooth pebbles. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Angular stones interlock and hold the sleepers firmly in place, and water drains between them.</details>

**35.** Every airliner window has a tiny hole in the inner pane. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It equalises pressure so the outer pane takes the cabin's pressure load, and it keeps the window from fogging.</details>

**36.** Why are manhole covers usually round?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A round cover can't fall into its own hole, whatever way it's turned.</details>

**37.** Large bridges have comb-like gaps at intervals. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>They are expansion joints, letting the structure grow and shrink with temperature without cracking.</details>

**38.** A flight recorder's 'black box' is painted bright orange. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>So that it's easy to spot in wreckage.</details>

## Classic Logic (39–63)

**39.** Three islanders: A says 'B is a knave.' B says 'C is a knave.' C says 'A and B are both knaves.' Knights always tell the truth; knaves always lie. Who is who?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A is a knave, B is a knight, C is a knave. If C were a knight, A and B would both be knaves; but then A's claim about B would be a lie that happens to be false, a contradiction. Testing the other cases leaves only this combination.</details>

**40.** Three prisoners stand in a line, all facing forward. A (at the back) sees B and C, B sees only C, and C sees nothing. There are three white hats and two black hats; each wears one. A says 'I don't know my colour.' B says 'I don't know mine.' What colour is C's hat, and how does C know?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>White. A would know his own colour only if B and C were both black, so they aren't. B then sees C: if C were black, B would know his own is white. B doesn't know, so C is white.</details>

**41.** Eight balls look identical, but one is slightly heavier. How can you find it with a balance scale in two weighings?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Weigh 3 against 3. If balanced, weigh the remaining 2 against each other. If not, take the heavier group of 3, weigh two of them, and the heavier or the one left out is the odd ball.</details>

**42.** 100 prisoners are interrogated one at a time in a room containing a light switch (initially off). When any prisoner is brought in they may toggle the switch. At any time one prisoner may declare 'Everyone has been in the room.' If he's right they all go free; if wrong, they are executed. How do they guarantee success?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Choose one prisoner to be the counter. Everyone else, the first time they find the light on... wait, they should only turn it on the first time they find it off, once only. The counter turns it off each time he finds it on and counts; at 99 he declares.</details>

**43.** You pick one of three doors; behind one is a prize. The host, who knows where it is, opens another door revealing nothing and offers you a switch. Should you?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. Your first pick wins with probability 1/3, so switching wins with 2/3.</details>

**44.** Three missionaries and three cannibals need to cross a river in a boat that holds two. Cannibals must never outnumber missionaries on either bank (if missionaries are present). How do they all cross?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>11 crossings: CC over, C back, CC over, C back, MM over, MC back, MM over, C back, CC over, C back, CC over.</details>

**45.** How many people do you need in a room for a better-than-even chance that two share a birthday?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>23 (about 50.7%).</details>

**46.** A soldier must cross a river. The only boat is at the bank with two boys. It can carry either the soldier or both boys, not more. How does everyone end up on the far side?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both boys cross; one returns; the soldier crosses; the boy on the far side returns the boat; both boys cross again.</details>

**47.** 100 prisoners each have a different number from 1 to 100. In a room are 100 boxes containing the numbers 1 to 100 shuffled randomly. Each prisoner may open 50 boxes, one at a time, to find their own number, and they all must succeed to be freed. They can agree a strategy beforehand. What strategy gives about a 31% chance?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Each prisoner opens the box with their own number, then the box number found inside, and so on. This follows a cycle in the permutation, and everyone succeeds when no cycle is longer than 50.</details>

**48.** Three ants sit on the corners of a triangle. Each randomly picks a side to walk along. What's the chance none collides?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>1/4. Only the two all-clockwise or all-anticlockwise choices out of 8 avoid collision.</details>

**49.** A toaster holds two slices, each side needs one minute. What's the fastest way to toast both sides of three slices?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Three minutes: toast A and B (side 1), then A (side 2) and C (side 1), then B (side 2) and C (side 2).</details>

**50.** You have two eggs and a 100-storey building. What's the fewest drops that guarantees finding the highest floor from which an egg survives?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>14. Drop first from floor 14, then climb 13, 12, 11... floors at a time; if the first egg breaks, use the second one floor at a time in the last interval.</details>

**51.** Five pirates (ranked by seniority) divide 100 gold coins. The most senior proposes a split; all vote, and if less than half agree, he is thrown overboard and the next proposes. Pirates are logical, greedy and want to survive. What does the senior pirate propose?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>98, 0, 1, 0, 1: he gives one coin each to the third and fifth pirates, who'd otherwise get nothing.</details>

**52.** Two opposite corners are cut from a standard 8×8 chessboard. Can the remaining squares be covered exactly by 31 dominoes?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. Opposite corners are the same colour, so the board has 32 of one colour and 30 of the other, but each domino covers one of each.</details>

**53.** At a party, a host and five couples (including the host and his wife) shake hands. Nobody shakes hands with their own partner. The host asks everyone how many hands they shook, and gets nine different answers: all numbers from 0 to 8. How many did the host's wife shake?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>4.</details>

**54.** Can you walk through the city of Königsberg, crossing each of its seven bridges exactly once?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. Euler showed that would need at most two land masses with an odd number of bridges, but all four had odd counts.</details>

**55.** 100 closed doors. Person 1 toggles every door, person 2 every second door, person 3 every third, and so on up to person 100. Which doors are left open?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The perfect squares: 1, 4, 9, ..., 100. Only those have an odd number of divisors.</details>

**56.** Join nine dots arranged in a 3×3 grid using four straight lines drawn without lifting the pen. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Extend the lines beyond the edges of the grid: a zigzag that goes outside the square.</details>

**57.** How many times a day do a clock's hour and minute hands overlap?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>22 times.</details>

**58.** 41 people stand in a circle and every third person is eliminated until one is left. Where should you stand to survive?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Position 31 (the classic Josephus problem).</details>

**59.** In the Tower of Hanoi, how many moves does it take to move n discs?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>2^n − 1. For 5 discs it's 31 moves.</details>

**60.** You have 25 horses and can race only 5 at a time, with no stopwatch. What's the fewest races needed to find the top three?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>7. Five heats; race the five winners; then race the five horses who could still be 2nd or 3rd (A2, A3, B1, B2, C1).</details>

**61.** A hundred people with blue eyes live on an island where nobody knows their own eye colour and nobody may discuss it. A visitor announces, 'At least one of you has blue eyes.' Anyone who works out their colour must leave that night. What happens?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>All 100 blue-eyed islanders leave on the 100th night. If there were one, he'd leave on night one; each extra person delays by a night.</details>

**62.** A mother says she has two children, and at least one is a boy. What is the probability both are boys?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>1/3 (the possibilities are BB, BG, GB).</details>

**63.** In complete darkness, you are handed 50 coins, 10 of which are heads-up. You can't tell which are heads. How do you split them into two piles with an equal number of heads?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Make a pile of 10 and a pile of 40, then flip every coin in the pile of 10.</details>

## Words & Wordplay (64–75)

**64.** I am an odd number. Take away one letter and I become even. What number am I?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>SEVEN (take away the S and you get EVEN).</details>

**65.** What common English word contains all five vowels in order?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Facetious.</details>

**66.** What word has a mile between its first and last letters?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>SMILES.</details>

**67.** I start with an E and end with an E, yet I contain only one letter. What am I?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>An envelope.</details>

**68.** What word, written in capitals, reads the same when turned upside down?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>SWIMS (rotated 180 degrees).</details>

**69.** What comes next? J, F, M, A, M, J, J, A, S, O, N, ?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>D. They're the first letters of the months.</details>

**70.** I am a five-letter word. Remove four of my letters and I still sound the same. What am I?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Queue.</details>

**71.** I am taken from a mine and shut in a wooden case from which I am never released, yet almost everyone uses me. What am I?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Pencil lead (graphite).</details>

**72.** What building has the most stories?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A library.</details>

**73.** Where does Friday come before Thursday?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>In a dictionary.</details>

**74.** What gets bigger the more you take away?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A hole.</details>

**75.** What goes up and down without moving?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A staircase.</details>

## Brain-Twisters & Paradoxes (76–100)

**76.** A woman sees a scene unfold: shattered glass flies up and rejoins into a window, smoke is sucked back into a chimney, a man walks backwards out of a building. She's perfectly calm. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>She's watching a film played in reverse.</details>

**77.** A man is found dead in a locked, windowless cellar in the middle of summer. There's no wound, no gas, no poison, and only a half-empty cooler and a puddle of water beside him. How did he die?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>He used dry ice in an enclosed space. As it sublimated, carbon dioxide displaced the oxygen and he suffocated.</details>

**78.** An entire family wakes feeling unwell after a rainy night. There was no fire and no gas leak, but they had brought the charcoal barbecue indoors to keep it dry. What was the culprit?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Carbon monoxide from the smouldering charcoal.</details>

**79.** A boy drops a stone into a well and hears the splash after three seconds. He calculates 44 m deep using the fall formula. A teacher says he's wrong. Why, and what's closer?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>He ignored the time the sound took to travel back up. Accounting for it, the well is about 40.6 m deep.</details>

**80.** A man always picks lottery numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. A friend laughs, saying that's a terrible choice. Is the friend right?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Those numbers are as likely as any, but many people choose them. If they win, the prize would be shared among many, so unusual numbers give a better expected payout.</details>

**81.** A king tells a prisoner, 'Make a statement. If it's true, you'll be beheaded; if it's false, you'll be hanged.' The prisoner says one sentence, and the king can't punish him. What did he say?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>'You will hang me.' If true he should be beheaded, but then the statement would be false and he'd have to be hanged; if false, he'd be hanged, which makes it true.</details>

**82.** A barber shaves exactly those men in town who do not shave themselves. Who shaves the barber?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>No one can. If he shaves himself he shouldn't, and if he doesn't he must. There can be no such barber (Russell's paradox).</details>

**83.** A dealer offers a bronze coin stamped '45 BC'. An expert knows immediately that it's fake. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Nobody at that time knew they lived 'before Christ'. The BC/AD dating system wasn't invented until centuries later.</details>

**84.** A spoonful of wine is moved from a glass of wine into a glass of water, mixed, and a spoonful of the mixture is moved back. Which glass is now purer: the wine in water, or the water in wine?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Neither. Each glass has the same amount of the other liquid.</details>

**85.** One bullet is fired horizontally from a rifle while another is dropped from the same height at the same moment. Which hits the ground first?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both land at the same time, because horizontal motion doesn't affect the fall.</details>

**86.** A helium balloon floats in a closed car that accelerates forward. Which way does it move?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Forward, because the air is pushed backwards and the light balloon moves into the low-pressure region.</details>

**87.** You see lightning, and nine seconds later hear the thunder. About how far away was it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>About 3 km, since sound travels about 343 m/s.</details>

**88.** A spaceship moving at 99% of light speed switches on its headlights. How fast does the light move relative to the ship?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>At the speed of light, c. Light always travels at c in any frame.</details>

**89.** How tall must a wall mirror be for you to see your full body in it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Half your height, wherever you stand.</details>

**90.** A coin has landed heads nine times in a row. What's the probability the next flip is heads?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>1/2. Past flips don't affect the next.</details>

**91.** A casino offers a game: flip a coin until it lands tails. If it takes n flips you win 2^n dollars. What's the fair price to play?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>In theory, infinity: the expected win is infinite, yet people won't pay much (the St. Petersburg paradox).</details>

**92.** A woman says: 'I have two children, and one of them is a girl born on a Sunday.' What's the probability both are girls?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>13/27, not 1/2: the extra information shifts the probabilities.</details>

**93.** Hospital A has better survival rates than Hospital B for both mild and severe cases, yet worse survival overall. How?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Hospital A takes mostly severe cases. For example, A: 9/10 mild, 18/90 severe (27% overall); B: 72/90 mild, 1/10 severe (73% overall). This is Simpson's paradox.</details>

**94.** Achilles gives a tortoise a head start and runs ten times as fast. Each time he reaches where the tortoise was, it has moved on a bit. Does he ever catch it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. The infinite series of ever-smaller distances adds up to a finite value, so he overtakes in finite time.</details>

**95.** A hotel with infinitely many rooms is full. A new guest arrives. Then an infinite bus of new guests arrives. Can both be housed?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. Move every guest from room n to n + 1 for one new guest; to house an infinite bus, move guest n into room 2n and use the odd rooms.</details>

**96.** How can you cut a round cake into eight equal pieces with only three straight cuts?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Make two perpendicular cuts through the centre to get four wedges, then slice horizontally through the middle of the cake.</details>

**97.** There are 10 stacks of coins, each with 10 coins. All the coins in one stack weigh 1 g more than the 10 g others. Using a scale that gives exact weights, how can you identify that stack in a single weighing?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Take 1 coin from stack 1, 2 from stack 2, ... 10 from stack 10 and weigh them. The weight above the normal total (in grams) equals the heavy stack's number.</details>

**98.** You have a 7-minute and an 11-minute hourglass. How can you time exactly 15 minutes?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Start both. When the 7 runs out, flip it. When the 11 runs out (at 11 min), flip the 7 again; it will have 4 minutes of sand left and runs out at 15.</details>

**99.** How many squares of all sizes are on a standard 8×8 chessboard?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>204 (1² + 2² + ... + 8²).</details>

**100.** Does 0.999... (with the 9s repeating forever) equal 1?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. Since 1/3 = 0.333..., multiplying by 3 gives 1 = 0.999...</details>
